## Historical raids

<br>

Here are well-documented Allied examples that match the pattern: a small parachute-inserted team whose job was to wreck a specific installation (radar, waterworks, heavy-water plant, airfield, etc.) rather than seize and hold ground.

<br>

### Operation Colossus (10–11 February 1941)

Britain’s first airborne raid. 38 men of X Troop, 11th SAS Battalion, jumped from Whitleys near Calitri in southern Italy to demolish the Tragino aqueduct that fed the ports of Taranto, Bari and Brindisi. They blew one pier and a nearby road bridge. The water interruption was short-lived and the entire party was captured, but the raid proved paratroops could reach an inland target.

### <br>

### Operation Biting – Bruneval Raid (27–28 February 1942)

C Company, 2nd Parachute Battalion (about 120 men under Major John Frost) dropped onto the cliffs at Bruneval, France, to seize and dismantle a Würzburg radar. After a short firefight they extracted the key components by sea. Two British dead, six captured; the intelligence haul was decisive for RAF bomber tactics.

### <br>

### Operation Gunnerside (16–28 February 1943)

Six Norwegian SOE commandos (Company Linge) parachuted onto the Hardangervidda plateau, linked with the four-man Grouse/Swallow team already on the ground, skied to Vemork, climbed the gorge, and planted charges that wrecked the heavy-water electrolysis cells. No shots fired. Combined with later ferry sinking, it ended Germany’s usable heavy-water production.

### <br>

### Early SAS airfield raids, North Africa (November 1941 onward)

After the disastrous first parachute drop (Operation Squatter), L Detachment SAS switched to small jeep or LRDG-inserted parties, but several later teams still jumped. Typical size was 4–12 men who placed delayed charges in aircraft and fuel dumps at Gazala, Sirte, Tamet and other fields, destroying scores of aircraft on the ground.

### <br>

### Operation Chestnut (July 1943)

Two 10-man SAS parties parachuted into northern Sicily the night after the Husky landings to cut roads and communications inland. Results were limited by scattered drops and poor radios, but the concept—tiny airborne sabotage teams operating independently of the main invasion—was the same.

### <br>

### Jedburgh and OSS Operational Groups (1944)

Three-man Jedburgh teams (usually one American, one British, one French officer or NCO) and larger 15-man OSS OGs were routinely parachuted into France and the Low Countries to blow rail lines, power stations and bridges in concert with the Maquis. Individual teams were small; the cumulative effect was large.

**Note on scale.** “Small group” in this context usually meant a company or less (Colossus, Biting) down to a handful of men (Gunnerside, SAS sticks, Jedburghs). Larger airborne operations such as Pegasus Bridge or the D-Day drops were seizures, not sabotage raids. Operation Freshman (the 1942 glider attempt on Vemork) is often mentioned alongside Gunnerside but was a failure and used gliders rather than a free-fall drop.

<br>

**Nadzab Jump:** While the 1st Parachute Battalion did not jump as a unit, Australian artillerymen from the 2/4th Field Regiment completed a famous operational combat jump on 5 September 1943. They parachuted alongside their dismantled guns into Nadzab, New Guinea, to support an American parachute regiment.
